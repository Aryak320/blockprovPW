\section{Introduction}

Modern machine learning systems are increasingly trained, evaluated, 
and deployed on data whose origins are difficult to trace. Training 
examples may come from multiple datasets, web-scale crawls, weak 
supervision pipelines, user interactions, synthetic data generated 
by large language models, or several rounds of cleaning and transformation. 
As a result, when a model performs well or poorly on a task, it is often 
unclear which parts of the data are responsible for that performance. 
In other words, we may observe the final utility of a model, but we do 
not have a principled account of the provenance of that utility.

This lack of provenance is particularly problematic for explainability. 
Standard explanations usually focus on the model or on individual input 
features: they explain why a specific prediction was made, or which features 
influenced that prediction. However, they often do not explain where the 
relevant evidence came from, which groups of training or evaluation records 
supported the observed behavior, or how uncertainty in the underlying data 
propagates to the final performance measure. This becomes even more important 
when the data itself is uncertain, automatically generated, deduplicated, 
clustered, or retrieved through vector search. In such settings, explanations 
at the level of individual tuples may be too fine-grained, unstable, or computationally expensive.

The goal of this project is to develop a block-level provenance framework 
for explaining aggregate behavior in relational and vector databases, with 
machine learning performance as a motivating application. Instead of assigning 
provenance only to individual tuples, we group tuples into semantically 
meaningful blocks and reason over possible worlds at the block level. 
A block may represent a cluster of similar examples, a group of records 
coming from a common source, a semantic region in an embedding space, or 
a task-specific collection of examples that jointly influence a model's 
performance. This gives a compact and interpretable abstraction: rather 
than asking which individual tuples contributed to an aggregate outcome, 
we can ask which blocks of data were responsible.

A central feature of this approach is that the blocks need not be fixed 
independently of the task. For a chosen machine learning task, we can 
construct the blocks by selecting an appropriate embedding map and 
clustering or assignment function. For example, if the task is image 
classification, the embedding may capture visual or class-level similarity; 
if the task is text classification, it may come from sentence or document 
embeddings; if the task is regression or time-series forecasting, it may 
encode numerical, temporal, or domain-specific features. Thus, the choice 
of embedding becomes part of the explanation mechanism: by choosing an 
embedding that reflects the relevant notion of similarity for the task, 
the resulting blocks provide a meaningful provenance abstraction for the 
observed performance.

This perspective connects provenance, uncertainty, and representation 
learning. The database provides the formal machinery for possible-world 
semantics and aggregate provenance, while the embedding space provides a 
task-aware way to define the units of explanation. Block-level provenance 
can therefore serve two purposes at once. First, it reduces the complexity 
of tuple-level possible-world reasoning by lifting the semantics to blocks. 
Second, it produces explanations that are closer to how practitioners 
reason about data: performance is often affected not by a single record, 
but by a group of related records, a data source, a semantic cluster, or 
a region of the input distribution.

We present a possible-world semantics for provenance of aggregate comparisons 
in relational databases and then consider its extension to vector databases. 
The main idea is to use block-level abstractions to handle tuple-level uncertainty 
efficiently, while preserving a formal connection to the underlying tuple-level 
possible worlds. These blocks are obtained through deterministic semantic 
partitioning of variables, using an embedding function and a clustering or 
nearest-neighbor assignment method. The resulting framework allows us to explain 
aggregate outcomes, and eventually machine learning performance measures, 
in terms of the provenance of semantically meaningful blocks of data.

\subsection{Semantic Partitioning of Variables}

Let $\mathbf{X}$ be the universe of variables (tuples). Fix a set of attribute(s) $A$ that define semantic similarity. Let
\[
\phi^A: \mathbf{X} \to \mathbb{R}^d
\]
be a deterministic feature/extraction map that transforms each tuple $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{X}$ into a $d$-dimensional representation suitable for vector search. Let
\[
g: \mathbb{R}^d \to \{1, \dots, m\}
\]
be a deterministic clustering method (or approximate nearest neighbor assignment). We define a map for assigning blocks from a finite collection $\mathcal{B}$ as
\[
\kappa: \mathbf{X} \to \mathcal{B} = \{B_1, \dots, B_m\}, \quad \kappa(\mathbf{x}) = B_{g(\phi^A(\mathbf{x}))}.
\]

The semantic partition induced by $\kappa$ is the family $\mathcal{B} = \{B_j\}_{j=1}^m$ with
\[
B_j = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{X} \mid \kappa(\mathbf{x}) = B_j \}.
\]

We require that $\{B_j\}$ forms a partition of $\mathbf{X}$, i.e.,
\[
\bigcup_{j=1}^{m} B_j = \mathbf{X}, \quad B_i \cap B_j = \emptyset \text{ for } i \neq j.
\]

\color{red}
\paragraph{Choices for $\phi^A$ and $g$ (Vector-database perspective)}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Numeric attributes:} 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\phi(x)$: normalized numeric vector (z-score or min--max). 
            \item Similarity metric: Euclidean or Mahalanobis. 
            \item $g$: k-means, mini-batch k-means, or vector database indexing (e.g., Faiss, Annoy, HNSW).
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Categorical attributes:} 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Encode with learned embeddings or frequency/one-hot $\to \phi(x)$.
            \item Similarity metric: cosine similarity.
            \item $g$: k-means on embedding space, hierarchical clustering, or ANN-based retrieval.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Text attributes:} 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Use sentence or attribute embeddings (e.g., Sentence-BERT, transformer CLS) $\to \phi(x)$.
            \item Similarity metric: cosine similarity.
            \item $g$: k-means, HDBSCAN, or ANN clustering in embedding space.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Large-scale / approximate nearest neighbors:} 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Use LSH, Faiss, or HNSW for embeddings; let $g$ assign LSH buckets or ANN clusters.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Threshold-based semantics:} 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Use DBSCAN with distance threshold $\varepsilon$ so that dense semantic neighborhoods become blocks; outliers form singletons.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\color{black}

Ensure that the choice of metric and $g$ makes $\kappa$ deterministic (e.g., fixed initialization / random seed) to preserve reproducibility.

\subsection{Block-world mapping and lifted block-level possible-world semantics}

\begin{proposition}[Block-world mapping]\label{prop:bw_mappings}
Let $\mathcal{B} = \{B_1,\dots,B_m\}$ be a collection of blocks disjointly partitioning $\mathbf{X}$ such that
\[
\bigcup_{k=1}^m B_k = \mathbf{X}, \quad B_i\cap B_j=\varnothing\ \text{for }i\neq j,
\]
and $\mathbf{X}_b:=\mathcal{B}$ is the analog of $\mathbf{X}$ but for blocks.

Define the mapping
\[
f:\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})\longrightarrow \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_b),\qquad
f(W):=\{\,B\in\mathbf{X}_b \mid B\cap W\neq\varnothing\,\},
\]
which sends each tuple-level possible world $W\subseteq\mathbf{X}$ to the set $W_b\subseteq\mathbf{X}_b$ of blocks that contain at least one tuple of $W$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For every $W\subseteq\mathbf{X}$, the image $W_b=f(W)$ is unique.
  \item The mapping $f$ induces a partition of tuple-level worlds:
    for every $V\subseteq\mathbf{X}_b$,
    \[
      \mathcal{W}_V := \{\,W\subseteq\mathbf{X}\mid f(W)=V\,\},
    \]
    and the family $\{\mathcal{W}_V\}_{V\subseteq\mathbf{X}_b}$ partitions $\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})$.
  \item (Lifting of semantics) Let $(K,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbb{0}_K,\mathbb{1}_K,\delta,\ominus)$
    be the $m$-semiring of tuple-level semantics, $(M,+_M,\mathbb{0}_M)$ the additive monoid of aggregates, and $\mathdutchcal{u},\mathdutchcal{v}$ two aggregations. Define block-level annotations:
    \[
      \mathbf{x}_B^{(K)} = \bigoplus_{\mathbf{x}\in B} \mathbf{x}^{(K)}, \quad
      \mathbf{x}_B^{(M)} = \bigplus_{M, \mathbf{x}\in B} \mathbf{x}^{(M)},
    \]
    and induced aggregations $\widehat{\mathdutchcal{u}},\widehat{\mathdutchcal{v}}$ on $\mathbf{X}_b$. Then
    \[
      [\mathdutchcal{u}\oslash\mathdutchcal{v}]_{\mathrm{op}}
      =
      \bigoplus_{V\subseteq\mathbf{X}_b}
        \Bigl(
          \bigotimes_{B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{u}})} \mathbf{x}_B^{(K)}
          \otimes
          \bigotimes_{B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{v}})} \mathbf{x}_B^{(K)}
          \otimes
          \widehat{\chi}_{\mathrm{op}}\!\Bigl(\bigplus_{M,B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{u}})}\mathbf{x}_B^{(M)},
                                          \bigplus_{M,B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{v}})}\mathbf{x}_B^{(M)}\Bigr)
          \otimes
          \widehat{\Phi}_{V}
        \Bigr).
    \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

 \begin{proof}
 \begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Uniqueness of the image.}
 The mapping $f$ is defined deterministically: given a tuple-world $W\subseteq\mathbf{X}$,
 the set $f(W)=\{B\in\mathbf{X}_b\mid B\cap W\neq\varnothing\}$ is a well-defined subset of $\mathbf{X}_b$.
 Because $f$ is a function (each input $W$ is assigned exactly one output $f(W)$), the image $W_b$ is unique.

 \item \textbf{Fibres partition $\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})$.}
 For any $V\subseteq\mathbf{X}_b$ the fibre $\mathcal{W}_V=\{W\subseteq\mathbf{X}\mid f(W)=V\}$ collects exactly those tuple-worlds whose tuples lie precisely in the blocks indexed by $V$ (i.e. each block in $V$ contains at least one tuple of $W$ and no tuple of $W$ lies in a block outside $V$). Distinct $V$ produce disjoint fibres, and every tuple-world $W$ lies in the fibre of $f(W)$, hence the family $\{\mathcal{W}_V\}_{V\subseteq\mathbf{X}_b}$ is a partition of $\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X})$.

 \item \textbf{Lifting of semantics.}
 Start from the tuple-level possible-world expansion from your semantics:
   \begin{align*}
   &[\mathdutchcal{u}\oslash\mathdutchcal{v}]_{\mathrm{op}}
 =
 \bigoplus_{W\subseteq\mathbf{X}}
   \Gamma(W),
     \\ &\text{where }\Gamma(W)
 \coloneqq
   \Bigl(\bigotimes_{W\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{u})}\mathbf{x}^{(K)}\Bigr)
   \otimes
   \Bigl(\bigotimes_{W\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{v})}\mathbf{x}^{(K)}\Bigr)
   \otimes
   \chi_{\mathrm{op}}\!\Bigl(\bigplus_{M,W\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{u})}\mathbf{x}^{(M)},
                           \bigplus_{M,W\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{v})}\mathbf{x}^{(M)}\Bigr)
   \otimes
   \Phi_{W}.
 \end{align*}
 Partition the outer sum according to the fibres of $f$:
 \[
 \bigoplus_{W\subseteq\mathbf{X}} \Gamma(W)
 =
 \bigoplus_{V\subseteq\mathbf{X}_b}\ \bigoplus_{W\in\mathcal{W}_V} \Gamma(W).
 \]
 This is merely a reorganization of the sum using the partition $\{\mathcal{W}_V\}$ established above.

 It remains to show that each inner sum $\bigoplus_{W\in\mathcal{W}_V}\Gamma(W)$ can be expressed in terms of block-level annotations
 $\mathbf{x}_B^{(K)}$ and $\mathbf{x}_B^{(M)}$. By the definitions
 \[
 \mathbf{x}_B^{(K)}=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{x}\in B}\mathbf{x}^{(K)}
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \mathbf{x}_B^{(M)}=\bigplus_{M,\;\mathbf{x}\in B}\mathbf{x}^{(M)},
 \]
 the product over all tuple-level annotations in $W\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{u})$
 can be regrouped block-wise. More precisely, because the blocks are disjoint,
 for $W\in\mathcal{W}_V$ the set $W\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{u})$ is equal to
 \[
 \bigcup_{B\in V}\bigl(B\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{u})\bigr),
 \]
 and therefore
 \[
 \bigotimes_{W\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{u})}\mathbf{x}^{(K)}
 =
 \bigotimes_{B\in V}
   \Bigl(\bigotimes_{\mathbf{x}\in B\cap\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{u})}\mathbf{x}^{(K)}\Bigr).
 \]
 Applying the semiring addition $\oplus$ inside each block (which is how we define $\mathbf{x}_B^{(K)}$)
 and using distributivity in the $m$-semiring,
 the inner combination over all tuple-worlds $W$ mapping to the same block-world $V$ collapses to an expression
 involving only the block-level annotations and block-level aggregate values.
 Analogous regrouping applies to the $M$-sums used inside $\chi_{\mathrm{op}}$.

 Hence each inner sum $\bigoplus_{W\in\mathcal{W}_V}\Gamma(W)$ can be rewritten as a block-level term of the form
 \[
 \Bigl(\!\bigotimes_{B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{u}})} \mathbf{x}_B^{(K)}\Bigr)
 \otimes
 \Bigl(\!\bigotimes_{B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{v}})} \mathbf{x}_B^{(K)}\Bigr)
 \otimes
 \widehat{\chi}_{\mathrm{op}}\!\Bigl(\bigplus_{M,B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{u}})}\mathbf{x}_B^{(M)},
                                 \bigplus_{M,B\in V\cap\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\mathdutchcal{v}})}\mathbf{x}_B^{(M)}\Bigr)
 \otimes
 \widehat{\Phi}_{V},
 \]
 where $\widehat{\chi}_{\mathrm{op}}$ tests the block-level aggregated quantities and $\widehat{\Phi}_{V}$
 is the block-level counterpart of the outside-world mask. Substituting these block-level terms back into the outer sum yields the claimed block-level expression.

 This completes the proof.

 \end{enumerate}
 \end{proof}

 \begin{remark}

 \begin{itemize}
   \item The partition (disjointness) hypothesis on the blocks is crucial for the simple form of the map $f$ and the algebraic regrouping. If blocks overlap (e.g. in certain storage/replication layouts) the mapping still exists but the algebraic lifting requires careful duplication-accounting.
   \item The equalities above are algebraic reorganisations inside the $m$-semiring and the monoid $(M,+_M)$. From an implementation viewpoint this is precisely why block-level possible-worlds can be used as a compact surrogate for tuple-level worlds, and why lazy expansion (reconstructing tuple-level annotations inside a block on demand) is sound: the block-level expression subsumes (by the fibres) the contributions of all tuple-level worlds lying under a given block-world.
 \end{itemize}
 \end{remark}

\paragraph{Example}
We illustrate Proposition~\ref{prop:bw_mappings} on a tiny relation with four tuples.  
Assume the tuples are partitioned into two semantic blocks:
\[
B_1=\{t_1,t_2\}, \qquad B_2=\{t_3,t_4\}.
\]
For simplicity, let the tuple annotations be independent Boolean provenance variables
\[
t_1:x_1,\quad t_2:x_2,\quad t_3:x_3,\quad t_4:x_4,
\]
and suppose the aggregate attribute is \texttt{salary} with values shown below.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
Tuple & Block & Salary & Annotation \\
\midrule
$t_1$ & $B_1$ & $50$ & $x_1$ \\
$t_2$ & $B_1$ & $70$ & $x_2$ \\
$t_3$ & $B_2$ & $40$ & $x_3$ \\
$t_4$ & $B_2$ & $30$ & $x_4$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Consider the query
\[
[\mathrm{SUM}(\texttt{salary}) > 100].
\]

At block level, we aggregate annotations and measure values inside each block:
\[
x^{(K)}_{B_1}=x_1 \oplus x_2,\qquad x^{(K)}_{B_2}=x_3 \oplus x_4,
\]
and
\[
x^{(M)}_{B_1}=50+70=120,\qquad x^{(M)}_{B_2}=40+30=70.
\]

The block-worlds are the subsets of $\{B_1,B_2\}$:
\[
\varnothing,\ \{B_1\},\ \{B_2\},\ \{B_1,B_2\}.
\]
For each block-world $V$, we evaluate the block-level aggregate and the comparison predicate
\[
\chi^b_{>}\!\left(\sum_{B\in V} x_B^{(M)},\,100\right).
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3cm}ccc}
\toprule
Block-world $V$ & Block sum & $\chi^b_{>}$ & Provenance contribution \\
\midrule
$\varnothing$ & $0$   & false & $0_K$ \\
$\{B_1\}$     & $120$ & true  & $x^{(K)}_{B_1}\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_1\}}$ \\
$\{B_2\}$     & $70$  & false & $0_K$ \\
$\{B_1,B_2\}$ & $190$ & true  & $x^{(K)}_{B_1}\otimes x^{(K)}_{B_2}\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_1,B_2\}}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Hence only the block-worlds $\{B_1\}$ and $\{B_1,B_2\}$ satisfy the predicate. Therefore the result annotation is
\[
[\mathrm{SUM}(\texttt{salary}) > 100]
=
\left(x^{(K)}_{B_1}\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_1\}}\right)
\oplus
\left(x^{(K)}_{B_1}\otimes x^{(K)}_{B_2}\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_1,B_2\}}\right).
\]

Substituting the block annotations gives
\[
[\mathrm{SUM}(\texttt{salary}) > 100]
=
\bigl((x_1\oplus x_2)\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_1\}}\bigr)
\oplus
\bigl((x_1\oplus x_2)\otimes (x_3\oplus x_4)\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_1,B_2\}}\bigr).
\]

This is exactly the lifted block-level view of the tuple-level possible worlds: instead of enumerating all tuple subsets of
$\{t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4\}$, we enumerate only block subsets of $\{B_1,B_2\}$ and use block-level aggregate values.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Block $B_1$ alone already makes the predicate true because its total salary is $120>100$.
Block $B_2$ alone does not, since $70 \not> 100$.
Thus the semantics cleanly separates the aggregate test from the provenance mass carried by each block.

\paragraph{Example: non-monotone aggregate comparison}
We now illustrate the same block-level construction for 
a non-monotone query.
Consider again the relation with two semantic blocks:
\[
B_1=\{t_1,t_2\}, \qquad B_2=\{t_3,t_4\},
\]
with tuple annotations
\[
t_1:x_1,\quad t_2:x_2,\quad t_3:x_3,\quad t_4:x_4.
\]

Consider the query
\[
[\mathrm{SUM}(\texttt{salary}) < 100].
\]

This query is non-monotone: adding more tuples or blocks 
can make a previously true query become false.

At block level, the annotations and aggregate values are
\[
x^{(K)}_{B_1}=x_1 \oplus x_2,\qquad 
x^{(K)}_{B_2}=x_3 \oplus x_4,
\]
and
\[
x^{(M)}_{B_1}=50+70=120,\qquad 
x^{(M)}_{B_2}=40+30=70.
\]

The block-worlds are the subsets of $\{B_1,B_2\}$:
\[
\varnothing,\ \{B_1\},\ \{B_2\},\ \{B_1,B_2\}.
\]

For each block-world $V$, we evaluate
\[
\chi^b_{<}\!\left(\sum_{B\in V} x_B^{(M)},\,100\right).
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3cm}ccc}
\toprule
Block-world $V$ & Block sum & $\chi^b_{<}$ & Provenance contribution \\
\midrule
$\varnothing$ & $0$   & true  & $\Phi^b_{\varnothing}$ \\
$\{B_1\}$     & $120$ & false & $0_K$ \\
$\{B_2\}$     & $70$  & true  & $x^{(K)}_{B_2}\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_2\}}$ \\
$\{B_1,B_2\}$ & $190$ & false & $0_K$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Hence the satisfying block-worlds are $\varnothing$ and $\{B_2\}$. Therefore,
\[
[\mathrm{SUM}(\texttt{salary}) < 100]
=
\Phi^b_{\varnothing}
\oplus
\left(x^{(K)}_{B_2}\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_2\}}\right).
\]

Substituting the block annotation for $B_2$ gives
\[
[\mathrm{SUM}(\texttt{salary}) < 100]
=
\Phi^b_{\varnothing}
\oplus
\left((x_3\oplus x_4)\otimes \Phi^b_{\{B_2\}}\right).
\]

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The query is satisfied when no block is present, since 
the sum is $0<100$, and it is also satisfied when only 
$B_2$ is present, since $70<100$. However, once $B_1$ 
is present, either alone or together with $B_2$, the 
sum becomes at least $120$ and the predicate becomes false. 
This demonstrates the non-monotonicity of less-than aggregate 
comparisons: adding more data can invalidate the query result.
